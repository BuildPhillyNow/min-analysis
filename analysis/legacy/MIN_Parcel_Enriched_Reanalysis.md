# The Mixed-Income Neighborhoods Overlay: A Parcel-Level Impact Analysis

**Build Philly Now | March 2026**

---

## Executive Summary

Philadelphia's Mixed-Income Neighborhoods (MIN) overlay imposed a 20% affordable housing requirement at 40% AMI, with no public subsidy, on any residential development of 10 or more units in portions of Council Districts 3 and 7. It took effect July 18, 2022.

This analysis examines 8,924 building permits (January 2019 – September 2025) enriched with OPA parcel data (98.9% address match) and L&I permit outcome statuses (100% match). Every methodological choice is deliberately conservative.

**Core finding:** Large residential projects inside MIN declined at an annualized rate of **-36.2 percentage points** more than the same districts' control areas — from 35 permits/year to 16/year inside MIN, versus 10.7/year to 8.8/year outside. Approximately **2,700 housing units** were never built, including an estimated **540 affordable units** that would have been required under MIN's own 20% set-aside. The policy produced **5 affordable units** in practice.

The existing TOD overlay (500 feet around 13 MFL stations) shows no evidence of counteracting MIN's chilling effect. Near-transit development collapsed just as severely within the actual TOD boundaries. The binding constraint is the unfunded affordability mandate, not height or parking — which is directly relevant to the Transit-Oriented Communities bill now before Council.

---

## 1. The Core Finding: A 36-Point Collapse

### Difference-in-Differences Design

The analysis compares permits *inside* the MIN overlay to permits *outside* MIN but within the same council districts (CD3 and CD7). This within-district design controls for neighborhood-level trends, political attention, demographics, and market cycles. The only systematic difference between the two groups is MIN exposure.

### Annualized Results

Because the pre-MIN period (3.54 years) and post-MIN period (3.18 years) differ in length, annualized rates provide a fairer comparison than raw counts:

| | Pre-MIN | Post-MIN | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Inside MIN** | 35.0 large permits/yr | 16.0/yr | **-54.2%** |
| **Outside MIN** | 10.7 large permits/yr | 8.8/yr | -18.0% |
| **MIN effect (DiD)** | | | **-36.2 pp** |

The -18.0% decline outside MIN captures market-wide headwinds (interest rates, construction costs, post-COVID normalization). The additional -36.2 points inside MIN is the policy's measured impact.

### Unit Loss Estimate

All methods use a **0% growth counterfactual** — flat continuation of the pre-MIN rate, with no assumed market growth. This is deliberately conservative. Some outside-MIN growth may itself reflect developer flight from MIN zones (spillover contamination), which would make a growth-based counterfactual overstate the impact. The 0% baseline is further supported by CD3's control group, where large permits were exactly flat at 24 pre-MIN and 24 post-MIN.

| Method | Lost Units | Description |
|--------|-----------|-------------|
| **Permit count method** | **~2,700** | 594 lost permits × 4.5 avg units. Uses all permits regardless of unit count or status. Most conservative. |
| Completed-unit method | 4,691 | Projects pre-MIN delivery rate (1,753 units/yr) forward. Upper bound — does not subtract market headwinds. |
| DiD-adjusted | 1,626 | Subtracts the control group's own -55% decline. Isolates the MIN-attributable effect, but the control sample is small (38→28 permits). |

**Headline figure: ~2,700 lost units.** For context, the pre-MIN delivery rate inside MIN was 1,753 completed large-project units per year. Post-MIN, it collapsed to 278 per year — an 84% decline.

## 2. District Breakdown: CD7 Was Hit Hardest

| Metric | CD3 Inside | CD3 Outside | CD7 Inside | CD7 Outside |
|--------|-----------|-------------|-----------|-------------|
| All permits | -21.7% | -6.2% | **-45.5%** | **+23.5%** |
| Large projects (10+u) | -50.8% | 0.0% | **-66.7%** | -71.4% |
| Entity/Developer permits | -43.8% | -13.7% | **-51.9%** | -3.5% |

CD7 shows the starkest divergence: overall permits inside MIN dropped 45.5% while *growing* 23.5% outside. In CD3, the large-project DiD is also clear: 61→30 inside versus 24→24 outside — a 50.8 pp differential within the district alone.

**Completed large-project units by district:**

| District | Pre-MIN | Post-MIN | Decline |
|----------|---------|----------|---------|
| CD3 | 3,455 units (51 permits) | 324 units (17 permits) | -91% |
| CD7 | 2,750 units (52 permits) | 559 units (14 permits) | -80% |

University City (proxied by CMX-4/5 zoning in CD3) went from 13 large permits (2,189 units) pre-MIN to 4 permits (1,059 units) post-MIN — and only 3 of those 4 are still "Issued," not yet completed. The rest of CD3 inside MIN (non-CMX-4/5) dropped 62% in completed units.

## 3. Hot Submarket Differential: 79 Percentage Points

Addresses with 3 or more total permits over the study period represent the most actively developed sites. These "hot submarkets" show the most extreme divergence:

| | Inside MIN | Outside MIN | Differential |
|---|---|---|---|
| Combined | -28.3% | **+50.6%** | **78.9 pp** |
| CD3 | -16.0% | +53.0% | 69.0 pp |
| CD7 | **-61.2%** | +49.0% | **110.2 pp** |

The active development sites where builders were most engaged collapsed inside MIN while equivalent sites outside MIN surged. In CD7, the hot submarket swing exceeds 110 percentage points.

---

## 4. Developer Activity Flipped

Entity and developer permits (LLC, LP, Corp) tell the clearest activity-shift story:

| Period | Inside MIN | Outside MIN | Share Inside |
|--------|-----------|-------------|-------------|
| Pre-MIN | 1,171 | 866 | 57.5% |
| Post-MIN | 613 | 773 | 44.2% |

Entity/developer activity inside MIN fell 47.7% while falling only 10.7% outside — a **37 pp differential**. The majority of professional development activity shifted from inside MIN to outside.

However, only 16 specific developers (2.4% of those active inside MIN pre-policy) were identified building outside MIN post-policy. The effect is less "flight" and more "freeze" — developers stopped building entirely rather than relocating.

## 5. Near-Transit Development Collapsed

Large projects inside MIN declined most severely in the transit-adjacent bands where the city most wants density:

| Distance to BSL/MFL | Inside MIN Change | Outside MIN Change |
|--------------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| <0.25 mi | -46% (35→17) | — (4→0) |
| 0.25-0.5 mi | -41% (34→18) | -50% (10→5) |
| 0.5-1.0 mi | -77% (44→9) | -37% (19→12) |
| >1.0 mi | +457% (1→5) | +100% (4→8) |

All-permit comparison near transit (<0.25 mi) captures the full picture:
- Inside MIN: 438 → 389 permits (-11.2%)
- Outside MIN: 280 → 376 permits (+34.3%)
- **45.5 percentage point swing** — development near transit grew outside MIN and shrank inside it.

## 6. The TOD Overlay Did Not Help

Philadelphia's existing TOD overlay (§14-513) applies within **500 feet** of specifically designated MFL stations. It provides height bonuses, increased FAR, up to 50% more dwelling units, and reduced parking. There are 13 designated stations; 12 overlap with MIN.

### Station Designation Timeline

| Era | Stations | Introduced By |
|-----|---------|--------------|
| Pre-2018 (Bill 170986) | 46th St, Spring Garden | — |
| 2018 (Bill 180008-A) | Berks, Allegheny, Somerset, Huntingdon, Tioga, Erie-Torresdale, Frankford TC | Quiñones Sánchez |
| ~2022 (Bill 220452) | 52nd, 56th, 60th, 63rd | Gauthier |

Most TOD stations were designated well before MIN took effect. The Sánchez stations (2018) had 4+ years of operation before MIN. Gauthier's 4 western MFL stations were introduced May 2022, roughly contemporaneous with MIN (July 2022).

### Results Within Actual TOD Boundaries

Only 171 of 8,924 permits (1.9%) fall within the actual 500-foot TOD overlay. Nearly all activity concentrates at Berks Station (42 permits).

| Period | Large Projects | Units |
|--------|---------------|-------|
| Pre-MIN | 9 permits | 435 units |
| Post-MIN | 2 permits | 61 units |
| **Change** | **-78%** | **-86%** |

The 2 post-MIN large projects (1924 N Front St, 32u; 1801-03 N Howard St, 29u) are both at Berks Station — the same developer corridor that was active pre-MIN. No new developers entered any TOD zone post-MIN.

**Gauthier's 4 western MFL stations (52nd–63rd) have generated zero building permits — zero pre-MIN, zero post-MIN.** The 500-foot radius captures very little developable land in these areas.

### Transit Proximity vs. TOD Overlay

The finding that near-transit parcels (within 0.5 miles) declined less than far-from-transit parcels is a **general land value effect**, not a TOD overlay effect. Transit-adjacent land in University City commands higher rents and can better absorb MIN's affordability cost. This has nothing to do with the TOD's height and parking provisions.

### What This Means for TOC

The current TOD demonstrates that **height and parking concessions within a 500-foot radius cannot overcome an unfunded 20% affordability mandate at 40% AMI**. A wider radius (quarter-mile) would capture more developable land, but unless TOC legislation modifies or supersedes MIN's affordability requirement — through subsidy, a lower set-aside percentage, a higher AMI target, or an opt-out — the same dynamic will repeat at a larger scale.

## 7. No Evidence of 9-Unit Threshold Gaming

Are developers downsizing projects to exactly 9 units to stay below MIN's 10-unit trigger?

**No.** Post-MIN inside MIN, there were only **4** nine-unit projects and **5** eight-unit projects. The ratio of just-under (7-9 unit) to just-over (10-12 unit) projects actually *declined* from 2.28 pre-MIN to 1.08 post-MIN. Developers are not gaming the threshold. They are simply not building.

---

## 8. Market-Rate Analysis: Excluding Subsidized Projects

14 large projects matched to the city's affordable housing inventory (HACE, Project HOME, Impact Services, Pennrose, NewCourtland). After excluding them:

**Market-rate large-project DiD:** Inside MIN 119→49 (-58.8%) vs. Outside MIN 37→22 (-40.5%) = **-18.3 pp**

The market-rate DiD is narrower than the all-project figure because the control group also declined more sharply for market-rate projects. Completed market-rate large-project units inside MIN fell from 6,013 pre to 835 post (**-86%**).

Zero large subsidized projects were completed inside MIN post-policy. One (Frankford Senior Housing, 50 units) remains "Issued." Pre-MIN, 5 large subsidized projects completed inside MIN. MIN deterred even affordable housing developers — the constituency the policy was designed to help.

## 9. The Abatement Rush Strengthens the Finding

Q4 2021 saw 29 large permits filed inside MIN — the tax abatement deadline rush. These 38 rush-window permits (Q4 2021 – Q2 2022) represent 31% of all pre-MIN large permits.

**These were real projects.** Rush-era permits completed at 71% (27 of 38), delivering 1,942 of 2,738 units. The normal pre-MIN completion rate was 88%. The 7 still-issued rush permits (590 units, including 4145 Chestnut 136u, 4240 Chestnut 128u, 2401 N 2nd 102u, 4400 Market 100u) represent projects with significant predevelopment investment.

**Including the rush is conservative.** It inflates the pre-MIN baseline, making the post-MIN collapse look smaller. Excluding the rush widens the DiD to **-52.7 pp**:

| Pre-MIN Window | Inside MIN | Outside MIN | DiD |
|---------------|-----------|-------------|-----|
| Full (2019 – Jul 2022) | 124→51 (-58.9%) | 38→28 (-26.3%) | -32.6 pp |
| Excluding rush (2019 – Sep 2021) | 86→51 (-40.7%) | 25→28 (+12.0%) | **-52.7 pp** |
| **Annualized (full)** | **35.0→16.0/yr (-54.2%)** | **10.7→8.8/yr (-18.0%)** | **-36.2 pp** |

## 10. Parcel Characteristics of the Collapse

Large-project parcels inside MIN shifted dramatically post-MIN:

| Characteristic | Pre-MIN (117 parcels) | Post-MIN (49 parcels) |
|---------------|----------------------|----------------------|
| Median lot size | 9,652 sqft | 7,922 sqft |
| Median market value | $3,749,050 | $1,598,000 |
| Entity/Developer share | 79% | 69% |
| Individual share | 5% | 20% |

The highest-value parcels stopped developing. Post-MIN large projects skew toward smaller lots and lower-value properties, suggesting only marginal projects proceed.

**Zoning codes that collapsed** (large permits, inside MIN):

| Zoning | Pre-MIN | Post-MIN | Change |
|--------|---------|----------|--------|
| CMX-2.5 | 19 | 3 | -84% |
| CMX-2 | 22 | 7 | -68% |
| CMX-3 | 8 | 2 | -75% |
| CMX-4 | 10 | 4 | -60% |
| CMX-5 | 3 | 0 | -100% |
| IRMX | 15 | 5 | -67% |

IRMX — specifically designed to encourage industrial-to-residential conversion — collapsed 67%. The city upzoned these parcels for density, and MIN made the upzoning uneconomic.

## 11. Recommendations: Fund It or Kill It

MIN's core design flaw is not the goal of mixed-income neighborhoods — it is the expectation that private developers will absorb the full cost of below-market units with no public contribution. At 40% AMI and current construction costs, the gap between market rents and affordable rents in University City or Kensington makes the math impossible for most projects. The result is predictable and now empirically demonstrated: developers don't build.

Other cities have learned this lesson. Philadelphia should learn from them.

### Portland, Oregon: The Before-and-After Story

Portland's inclusionary housing program (2017) initially required 20+ unit projects to set aside affordable units — unfunded, like MIN. The results were Philadelphia's results: multifamily permitting fell 40% from its 2016 peak, and the share of projects sized just below the 20-unit threshold doubled.

In February 2024, Portland and Multnomah County voted to **fully fund** the program — providing roughly $220,000 per below-market unit to offset the gap between market and affordable rents. The program is now estimated to yield approximately 300 rent-restricted homes per year. By 2025, Portland approved more housing units than any year on record, and the share of undersized threshold-gaming projects returned to normal.

### Oregon SB 1521: Unfunded IZ is Now Illegal

On March 4, 2026, Oregon's legislature passed SB 1521, requiring Portland-area jurisdictions that impose inclusionary zoning mandates to keep them **fully funded** through countervailing subsidies or tax breaks. Unfunded inclusionary zoning — the category MIN falls into — is on track to be illegal in Oregon's largest metro area. Oregon is the first state to legislate this distinction.

### Baltimore: The Cost of Not Funding

Baltimore's original inclusionary housing law (2007–2022) required 20% affordable units — with a waiver if the city couldn't fund the gap. The city never had the money. The result: **34 affordable units in 14 years**, while $38 million in annual tax subsidies for market-rate development produced zero affordable units. Baltimore reformed its law in 2024, but the lesson is the same: unfunded mandates produce waivers, not housing.

### Chicago: Fee-in-Lieu Leakage

Chicago's Affordable Requirements Ordinance (ARO) allows developers to pay a fee in lieu of building affordable units: $175,000/unit downtown, $50,000 in lower-income areas. Developers routinely pay the fee rather than build. Since 2007, the ARO has produced only 2,798 units, concentrated in the most expensive neighborhoods. The fee structure creates an incentive to avoid the mandate rather than meet it.

### What Philadelphia Should Do

1. **Fund the gap.** Calculate the actual difference between market rents and 40% AMI rents in MIN zones. At current U City market rents (~$2,200/month for a 1BR) versus 40% AMI (~$850/month), the annual per-unit gap is approximately $16,200. Over a 15-year compliance period, that is roughly $175,000–$225,000 per unit in present value — comparable to Portland's $220,000 subsidy. Philadelphia should commit public dollars (AHTF, tax abatement savings, PILOT revenue) to close this gap, as Portland and Oregon now require.

2. **Adjust the rates and ratios.** MIN's 20% at 40% AMI is among the most aggressive unfunded inclusionary requirements in the country. Portland's funded program offers flexibility between 10% at 60% AMI or 20% at 80% AMI. Philadelphia should align set-aside percentages and AMI targets with what the market can actually deliver, given funding levels.

3. **Expand the TOD radius.** The current 500-foot TOD overlay is too small to capture meaningful development potential. The TOC bill's quarter-mile radius is a better starting point, but it must be paired with modified affordability requirements — not layered on top of MIN's unfunded mandate.

4. **Require calibration studies.** Oregon SB 1521 requires jurisdictions to regularly evaluate whether their inclusionary programs are fully funded. Philadelphia should adopt the same self-evaluation requirement, ensuring that the affordability mandate never exceeds the available subsidy.

The fundamental question is not whether Philadelphia should promote mixed-income development — it should. The question is whether the cost of that goal falls entirely on the private sector (producing nothing, as MIN demonstrates) or is shared with the public (producing housing, as Portland now demonstrates). If Baltimore can commit to funding inclusionary housing, so can Philadelphia.

## Methodology

- **Data**: 8,924 L&I building permits, Council Districts 3 and 7, January 2019 – September 2025
- **Treatment**: Permits inside MIN overlay (spatial join against PCPC zoning shapefile)
- **Control**: Permits outside MIN, within the same council districts
- **Treatment date**: July 18, 2022 (City Council Bill 220519)
- **Large project threshold**: 10+ units (the MIN policy trigger)
- **Address match rate**: 98.9% to OPA via CARTO (6,571/6,641 unique addresses; 98.1% inside MIN, 99.5% outside)
- **Permit status match**: 100% from L&I CARTO `permits` table (8,924/8,924)
- **Unit count validation**: 100% agreement between source xlsx and L&I CARTO (4,272/4,272)
- **Transit analysis**: BSL + MFL station coordinates with Euclidean distance; TOD overlay via spatial join against PCPC Zoning_Overlays shapefile
- **Affordable housing matching**: 484 city-funded affordable housing projects matched by address (34 permit matches in CD3/CD7)
- **52% missing unit counts**: Unchanged from prior analysis. Status-based analyses use all permits; unit-based analyses use only permits with known units.
